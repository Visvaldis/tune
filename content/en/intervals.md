When you listen to music, you're hearing melodies and harmonies built from the distances between notes. These distances — called **intervals** — are one of the most fundamental building blocks of music. Learning to recognize intervals by ear unlocks a deeper understanding of how music works and how to play it.

> "Your ear learns intervals through repetition and recognition."

## What is an Interval?

An interval is the distance between two pitches, measured in **semitones** (also called half-steps). A semitone is the smallest distance between two notes in Western music — for example, from C to C♯, or from E to F.

Every interval has a specific character and sound quality. Some intervals sound consonant and stable, while others sound tense or dissonant. The ability to identify these sounds is called **ear training**, and it's a skill every musician develops over time.

## Common Intervals

Here are the essential intervals you'll encounter in most music, along with their semitone counts:

- **Unison** (0 semitones) — the same note played twice
- **Minor 2nd (m2)** (1 semitone) — a half-step, the smallest interval
- **Major 2nd (M2)** (2 semitones) — a whole step
- **Minor 3rd (m3)** (3 semitones) — a sad, introspective sound
- **Major 3rd (M3)** (4 semitones) — bright and cheerful
- **Perfect 4th (P4)** (5 semitones) — stable and open
- **Tritone** (6 semitones) — tense and unstable, exactly half an octave
- **Perfect 5th (P5)** (7 semitones) — strong and powerful
- **Octave (P8)** (12 semitones) — the same note, one octave higher

Each of these intervals has a unique emotional quality that composers use to shape melodies and harmonies.

## Famous Song References

One of the best ways to internalize intervals is to connect them with familiar songs. When you hear the first two notes of these melodies, you're hearing that interval:

- **Minor 2nd (m2)** — the ominous "Jaws" theme (1 semitone)
- **Major 2nd (M2)** — "Happy Birthday" (2 semitones)
- **Minor 3rd (m3)** — "Greensleeves" (3 semitones)
- **Major 3rd (M3)** — "When the Saints Go Marching In" (4 semitones)
- **Perfect 4th (P4)** — "Here Comes the Bride" (5 semitones)
- **Perfect 5th (P5)** — "Star Wars" main theme (7 semitones)
- **Octave (P8)** — "Somewhere Over the Rainbow" (12 semitones)

These reference songs act as mental anchors. When you're trying to identify an interval, recall the song that starts with it, and the recognition becomes instant.

## Why Intervals Matter

Intervals are the foundation of both **melody** and **harmony**:

- **In melody**, intervals determine the shape and emotional arc of a tune. A leap of a perfect 5th sounds heroic; a step of a minor 2nd sounds tense.
- **In harmony**, intervals combine to form chords. A major 3rd paired with a perfect 5th creates a major chord; a minor 3rd with a perfect 5th creates a minor chord.

Whether you're learning to play by ear, transcribe music, or improvise, interval recognition is essential.
