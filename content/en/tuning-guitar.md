Before you play a single chord or scale, your guitar must be in tune. An out-of-tune guitar makes every note sound wrong, no matter how well you play. Tuning is the first skill every guitarist learns, and it's something you'll do before every practice session.

## Standard Tuning

The standard tuning for a six-string guitar is **E-A-D-G-B-E**, from the lowest (thickest) string to the highest (thinnest). Guitarists number the strings from 6 (lowest) to 1 (highest):

- **6th string** — E (82.41 Hz)
- **5th string** — A (110.00 Hz)
- **4th string** — D (146.83 Hz)
- **3rd string** — G (196.00 Hz)
- **2nd string** — B (246.94 Hz)
- **1st string** — E (329.63 Hz)

These specific frequencies are based on the equal temperament system, with A4 set to 440 Hz. The 1st string E is two octaves above the 6th string E.

## Methods of Tuning

There are three main ways to tune your guitar:

**Electronic tuner.** This is the easiest and most accurate method for beginners. Clip-on tuners attach to your guitar's headstock and detect vibrations. They show you whether each string is too low (flat), too high (sharp), or perfectly in tune. The display usually has a needle or lights that center when the pitch is correct.

**Tuning by ear.** Once you have a reference tone — from a piano, pitch pipe, tuning fork, or another tuned instrument — you can adjust your guitar strings to match. This develops your ear over time and is essential when you don't have a tuner available.

**Relative tuning.** This method tunes the strings to each other, rather than to a fixed reference. It's useful when you need your guitar to sound good on its own, even if it's not at standard pitch.

## The 5th Fret Method

The most common relative tuning technique is the 5th fret method. It works like this:

1. Tune the 6th string (low E) to a reference, or just assume it's in tune.
2. Press the 5th fret of the 6th string — it should sound the same as the open 5th string (A). Adjust the 5th string until they match.
3. Press the 5th fret of the 5th string — it should match the open 4th string (D). Tune the 4th string.
4. Press the 5th fret of the 4th string — it should match the open 3rd string (G). Tune the 3rd string.
5. **Exception:** Press the **4th fret** of the 3rd string — it should match the open 2nd string (B). Tune the 2nd string.
6. Press the 5th fret of the 2nd string — it should match the open 1st string (E). Tune the 1st string.

This method works because pressing the 5th fret of a string produces the same note as the next thinner string when open — except for the 3rd to 2nd string transition, where you use the 4th fret.

## Tips for Tuning

**Always tune up.** If a string is sharp, tune it down below the target pitch, then tune up to the correct note. This prevents the string from slipping flat immediately after you think you're done.

**New strings stretch.** If you've just put on new strings, they'll go out of tune quickly at first. Tune them, play for a few minutes, and tune again. Repeat several times until they stabilize.

**Turn tuning pegs gently.** Small adjustments make a big difference in pitch. Turn the peg slowly and give the string a moment to settle after each turn.

**Check your tuning often.** Even during a practice session, strings can drift out of tune from temperature changes or playing. A quick check every 15-20 minutes keeps your ear training on track.

Tuning isn't just a chore — it's ear training. The more you tune by ear and learn to recognize when a string is sharp or flat, the better your overall sense of pitch becomes. With practice, you'll be able to tune your guitar quickly and confidently anywhere.
