Before you can play the guitar well, it helps to know its parts. Every component has a specific role in creating the rich, resonant sound that makes the guitar one of the world's most beloved instruments. From the wood that forms the body to the metal frets that guide your fingers, understanding each piece will make you a more confident player.

> "Every part of the guitar works together to create sound."

## The Body

The body is the largest part of an acoustic guitar. Its main component is the **soundboard** (also called the top), a thin wooden panel that vibrates when the strings move. In the center of the soundboard sits the **sound hole**, a circular opening that allows sound waves to escape and project outward.

The **back and sides** of the body are typically made from hardwoods like rosewood or mahogany. These pieces work with the soundboard to amplify vibrations and shape the guitar's tone. At the lower end of the body, you'll find the **bridge**, a flat piece of wood that anchors the strings. Sitting on top of the bridge is the **saddle**, a thin strip (usually bone or plastic) that sets the string height and transfers vibration energy into the soundboard.

## The Neck

The neck is the long, narrow piece extending from the body. It's what you grip with your fretting hand. The front surface of the neck is called the **fretboard** or fingerboard — a smooth, flat strip of wood (often rosewood or ebony) where you press down the strings.

Running perpendicular across the fretboard are the **frets**: thin metal strips that divide the neck into segments. Each fret represents one semitone, the smallest interval in Western music. When you press a string down just behind a fret and pluck it, you shorten the vibrating length of the string, raising its pitch.

Many guitars have small dots or decorative markers called **position markers** inlaid at certain frets (typically the 3rd, 5th, 7th, 9th, 12th, and sometimes 15th and 17th). These help you navigate the fretboard without having to count frets constantly.

## The Headstock

At the top of the neck is the **headstock**, a wider section that holds the **tuning pegs** (also called machine heads or tuners). There are six tuning pegs — one for each string. Turning a peg tightens or loosens its string, changing the pitch.

Between the headstock and the fretboard sits the **nut**, a small grooved strip (usually made of bone, plastic, or synthetic material) that spaces the strings evenly and sets their height above the first fret.

## The Strings

A standard guitar has six strings, each tuned to a specific pitch. From the thickest (lowest-sounding) to the thinnest (highest), the standard tuning is **E-A-D-G-B-E**. The three thickest strings (E, A, D) are usually **wound** — a thin core wire wrapped in another metal wire — while the thinner three (G, B, E) are plain steel or nylon.

## How Sound is Made

When you pluck a string, it vibrates. That vibration travels through the saddle into the bridge, which causes the soundboard to vibrate. The soundboard's movement pushes air inside the body, and those sound waves exit through the sound hole, projecting outward to your ears. The hollow body acts as a resonator, amplifying the relatively quiet vibration of a thin string into a sound loud enough to fill a room.

Every piece — from the nut to the bridge, from the frets to the tuning pegs — plays a role in that chain of vibration. When all parts are in good condition and properly adjusted, the guitar becomes a responsive, expressive instrument. Understanding this anatomy is the first step toward caring for your guitar and getting the best sound out of it.
